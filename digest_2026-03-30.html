🔵 Core Insights

The venture business structurally does not work — and that's by design

[20VC] The Venture Model is Broken | Gili Raanan • Listen →
Gili Raanan, founder of Cyberstarts and former Sequoia GP, argues that the venture business is not supposed to work for most players. Returns distribution is inherently unequal, and only a tiny number of firms (Sequoia, Andreessen, Benchmark, Greylock, Lightspeed) sustain performance over decades. With money flooding into the market, LPs who have distributed their venture allocation evenly should be worried.
"The expectation that a venture business would work out is set yourself for disappointment from the get-go. It doesn't work. Now, it worked for some people. It worked for some people for some time. And the number of people it works for over a long period of time is super small."

Multiple specialized AI agents beat one general-purpose agent

[Lenny] From Skeptic to True Believer: How OpenClaw Changed My Life | Claire Vo • Listen →
Claire Vo, former product executive and founder of ChatPRD, runs nine specialized OpenClaw agents across multiple Mac Minis and old laptops. Each agent handles a specific domain: family calendar, inbound sales, kids' homework help, podcast prep, and course management. The approach of dedicated agents per task outperforms trying to use a single AI for everything.
"Claire now runs her business, podcast, and family life with the help of nine OpenClaw agents running on multiple Mac Minis and old laptops... from OpenClaw skeptic (it deleted her family calendar the first time she tried it) to true believer."

The U.S. lost 9 out of 10 submarine manufacturing jobs after the Cold War

[A16Z] Submarines and the Future of Defense Manufacturing • Listen →
After the Cold War ended, the U.S. essentially walked away from submarine manufacturing. Production collapsed from four nuclear submarines per year in the mid-1980s to roughly three total in the entire 1990s. Nine out of ten manufacturing jobs vanished, and an entire generation was told to skip the factory floor. Now, the Navy needs more than five times the capacity it had a decade ago, and the workers who could fill those roles aged out with nobody replacing them.
"At the end of the Cold War, we walked away from manufacturing. We only built about three submarines in the 1990s... the amount of work that we need now to replenish our fleet is on the order of about 70 million hours."

Ron Conway's investing philosophy: be an advocate for founders, not a passive check-writer

[Uncapped] Ron Conway from SV Angel • Listen →
Ron Conway describes SV Angel's core operating principle: "advocates for founders" who are always on, whether it's 2 AM or in the middle of a crisis. This isn't passive angel investing — it means helping with UCSF hospital connections for sick family members, fighting the government during the SVB crisis, and rallying to reinstate Sam Altman at OpenAI. Conway views his relationship network as SV Angel's most valuable asset.
"If SV Angel had a moniker, it would be advocates for founders. We want to help the whole founder... If they have a problem at two in the morning and I'm in Venice, I'm picking up the phone."

Velocity and growth rate are the single best predictor of company success

[20VC] The Venture Model is Broken | Gili Raanan • Listen →
Gili Raanan argues that when a company grows exceptionally fast, it becomes part of their DNA. It doesn't just slow down because of averages — there needs to be a significant external event. His benchmark: 4x, 4x, 3x, 3x growth in new ARR over the first five years, which compounds to 144x. A company doing $1M in year one would book $144M in new ARR by year five.
"Whenever you see a business that's growing very, very fast, it's a good company... if a company grows fast, it would continue to grow fast. It's part of the DNA. They probably do something very right at that company."

Thematic investing is the only way to build conviction at the seed stage

[Uncapped] Ron Conway from SV Angel • Listen →
SV Angel has always been thematic investors, typically tracking about six themes at any given time. Any company fitting those themes gets serious consideration; everything outside gets quickly turned down. This approach has been consistent from the search engine era through B2B, internet, and now AI. Within AI, they've had to create sub-themes because everything is AI.
"We're always thematic. We have this piece of paper... we're always looking at probably six themes. Any company in that theme that comes in the door, we're going to at least take a look at. If it doesn't fit those themes, we pretty quickly turn them down."

The submarine industrial base is not a money problem — it's a people problem

[A16Z] Submarines and the Future of Defense Manufacturing • Listen →
Chris Power of Hadrian explains that the capital can be purchased, but the skilled workforce simply doesn't exist. The U.S. told kids in the '80s and '90s to skip manufacturing and get four-year degrees instead. Now the remaining skilled workers are in their late 50s and 60s, and training a Navy welder takes a decade. Software must augment workers to get 50-70% fewer people to produce the same output.
"We could spend $10 billion hiring 2 million welders that he needs. They just don't exist in the country. We have to get this productivity uplift by fusing workforce training and software together to go a lot faster."

A single accountable person in charge is the biggest unlock for defense procurement

[A16Z] Submarines and the Future of Defense Manufacturing • Listen →

"One of the smartest things we could be doing is, hey, there is a single accountable person that just runs this instead of 20 people trying to contribute... that's not possible unless there's a single man or woman in charge."

Product-market fit is a structured exercise, not a happy accident

[20VC] The Venture Model is Broken | Gili Raanan • Listen →

IPOs are marketing events, not liquidity events — they're actually the opposite of liquidity

[20VC] The Venture Model is Broken | Gili Raanan • Listen →
Raanan challenges the common assumption that IPOs are about financial liquidity. In reality, you get shackles on your hands, cannot sell stock, and face all kinds of limitations. "Going public is not a financial event. It's a branding event... It's hell for liquidity, but it's an important marketing event."

Let team members play to their strengths, not fix their weaknesses

[Uncapped] Ron Conway from SV Angel • Listen →
Conway's approach to building a great venture partnership is counterintuitive: instead of creating guardrails that bring everyone to the same mode of operation, he lets each team member play to their relative strengths. On weaknesses, the best they can be is average. On strengths, they create real differentiation.
"I would let each team member play on their relative strengths and would not require them to focus on improving their weaknesses... on their weaknesses, at the best case, they can be as good as the market. But on the things that are exceptional, they are creating real advantage."
