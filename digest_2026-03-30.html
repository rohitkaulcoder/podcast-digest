The Venture Model is Broken | You Need to be Greedy and Selfish to Win Early
20VC
Core

Seed investing requires selfishness and greed as virtues

Gilly Renan, former Sequoia GP and founder of CyberStarts, argues that early-stage investing demands a mindset most people consider negative. With his Fund 1 producing a decacorn (Wiz), seven unicorns, and three acquisitions from just 19 companies, the math works precisely because he's ruthlessly selective.

"We need to be selfish and we need to be greedy. Those are good traits for an early stage investor."

The T2D3 framework remains the north star for SaaS greatness

Triple-triple-double-double-double: 3x new ARR in year 2, then 3x again, then double three times. Starting with $1M new ARR, that yields $144M new ARR by year 5. The bar for what constitutes a great company hasn't changed, even if individual companies can exceed it.

Focus on portfolio, not coverage

Renan admits missing maybe one or two Israeli cyber seed deals in eight years, but deliberately refuses to stress about comprehensive coverage. Energy goes into existing portfolio companies, not chasing every deal.

"If you are stressed about winning every battle, I can predict you are not going to be in every important AI company."
Counter-Intuitive

Founder babysitting is a trap for seed investors

Unlike the prevailing "value-add VC" narrative, Renan explicitly says he's not in the business of babysitting founders. His model is high conviction entry, then let the founders execute. The hands-off approach contradicts the industry's trend toward operational support.

Old frameworks are dead but the instincts behind them still work

Younger VCs are told that rule-of-40, triple-triple-double-double, and margin focus are obsolete in the AI era. Renan's advice: learn from experienced investors but ultimately trust your gut. The frameworks may shift but the judgment muscle is timeless.

Data Points

Public market multiples have collapsed for SaaS

Monday.com trades at ~1.5x revenue. Wix at ~2.5x (announcing a buyback at $4B market cap). The expectation that autonomous AI agents will eat into these businesses is compressing multiples, even for companies with strong cash flows.

CyberStarts Fund 1: 19 companies, 1 decacorn, 7 unicorns

An extraordinary hit rate from a concentrated seed portfolio focused exclusively on Israeli cybersecurity. The concentrated thesis-driven approach produced returns most diversified funds can only dream of.

Future-Looking

AI will compress SaaS multiples further before it creates new ones

Markets are pricing in the expectation that autonomous programs will displace parts of existing SaaS businesses. Renan predicts this compression will continue before a new generation of AI-native companies redefines the category.

Core

Series A now requires billion-dollar funds

Round sizes at Series A have expanded so dramatically that firms without massive fund sizes simply can't write checks large enough to lead. The math on reserves, follow-on, and ownership targets demands scale that was previously associated with growth-stage investing.

Anthropic is eating OpenAI's lunch in the enterprise

Ramp's spending data suggests Anthropic is winning enterprise adoption over OpenAI. The panel discusses whether this is a durable trend or an artifact of OpenAI's consumer-first approach creating an enterprise opening.

The reserve ratio determines your strategy more than the entry check

For Series A-focused firms, reserving ~50% of initial capital for follow-on defines the game. Unlike seed investors who treat the A as an option on a bigger later check, pure A/B firms need the first check to be where most of the value is made.

Counter-Intuitive

DeepSeek was a "massive market overreaction to a proof of concept"

The panel pushes back hard on the DeepSeek panic, arguing that established players like Sequoia should not have been rattled. If you're a software company not already thinking about AI disruption to both how you build and what you build, you should be shorting yourself.

Antitrust enforcement has become a revenue extraction mechanism

Government antitrust policy is described as a "perverse incentive" where the outcome is always "wire me the money." Whether deals close or don't, the government collects through some combination of fines and taxes. Cleaning up the rules would cost the government $20-30B.

Future-Looking

The SpaceX and Tesla Playbook for Hard Tech Startups
The A16Z Show
Core

Question every requirement — simple is fast, simple is cheap

The core engineering lesson from SpaceX: relentlessly question requirements until you've eliminated every unnecessary one. What remains can be designed simply, built quickly, and manufactured cheaply. This is more than "move fast" — it's a systematic method for finding the shortest path to production.

The factory mindset: design for production from day one

SpaceX alumni describe a culture where engineering decisions are always evaluated through the lens of "can this be manufactured at scale?" A snorkel component designed for Booster was repurposed for Ship, skipping an entire design cycle. The trick was proving the edge case (liquid in the valves) rather than redesigning from scratch.

Vertical integration is a survival question, not an ideology

The useful framing isn't "should we vertically integrate?" but "does the company exist without it?" When a critical supply chain doesn't have enough competition to ensure cost reduction, you must own it. Otherwise, outsource.

Counter-Intuitive

Aggressive deadlines make teams think more deliberately, not less

Counter to the "move fast, break things" caricature: impossibly aggressive timelines at SpaceX force teams to identify the 100 things (out of 1,000) that genuinely can't be done in six months — and attack those first. Speed comes from ruthless prioritization, not recklessness.

Hiring slowly is a feature at companies known for speed

At Tesla, engineers talk to 6+ other engineers and go through 8-10 conversations before getting an offer. This technical rigor ensures new hires can be autonomous and balance authority with accountability. Slower hiring enables faster execution.

Data Points

SpaceX alumni have founded 100+ companies in two decades

From a starting point of 160 employees in 2002, SpaceX has produced a founder pipeline rivaling any accelerator. Tesla's alumni pipeline runs in parallel, concentrated in hard-tech sectors like minerals, energy, and defense.

Future-Looking

The SpaceX/Tesla alumni network will define the next era of hard-tech

Companies like Galadai (missile propulsion) and Mariana Minerals (critical mineral supply chains) represent a new generation applying SpaceX-style engineering rigor to defense and industrial problems. The playbook is becoming reproducible.

Security, Resilience, and the Future of Mobile Infrastructure
The A16Z Show
Core

Assume the infrastructure is hostile and build on top

CAPE's founding insight: rather than trying to find and remove Chinese infiltration from existing telecom infrastructure, build a clean software-defined cellular network on top of it. Treat the physical layer as compromised by default.

"Rather than trying to ferret through the existing carriers and find all the China and try to get rid of it, let's just do a clean install of a telco on top of the existing physical infrastructure."

The Navy's innovation model shifted from builder to gardener

The Navy CTO describes a transformation from trying to build everything internally to "planting seeds" across the ecosystem — identifying promising startups, running pilots, and using an Innovation Adoption Kit to scale successes across commands. Special ops serves as a proving ground for Big Navy.

Selling to defense means finding the one indispensable module

When a contract has 10 modules, 9 don't matter but 1 is indispensable. The advice: make tasks fully severable, then target the hardest piece. Don't try to solve the whole problem — own the chunk that can't be turned off.

Counter-Intuitive

Bad startup experience is worse than no startup experience

For veterans transitioning to tech, joining a bad startup teaches bad behaviors. The recommendation is to specifically seek out good startups because "learning what not to do" is not actually the useful lesson — learning what to do is.

Data Points

Salt Typhoon: only 5 of 60 cybersecurity experts at Davos knew about it

Despite being one of the most significant telecom breaches in history — China infiltrating every major US carrier's lawful intercept systems — awareness was shockingly low even among security professionals.

CAPE was building its solution three months before Salt Typhoon was publicly disclosed

The company had already identified the vulnerability pattern and begun building a resilient alternative before the breach became public knowledge, validating their thesis about compromised telecom infrastructure.

Future-Looking

Software-defined telecom will become a national security imperative

As the scope of state-sponsored telecom compromise becomes clearer, clean-install software-defined networks will move from experimental Navy deployments to critical infrastructure requirements. The model of treating hardware as untrusted is the future.

What Nobody Tells Engineers About Becoming a CEO
In Depth
Core

The CEO operates in a "fog of partial understanding"

Jay Kreps, co-founder of Confluent, describes the hardest transition: from engineering where problems are mostly knowable, to CEO where you make critical decisions with unknowable aspects. You must be roughly directionally right rather than precisely correct.

"Very quickly as the organization gets bigger, it's impossible to know everything about everything. You have to kind of be roughly directionally right."

A single blog post did more than years of engineering for Kafka adoption

Kreps realized that if they couldn't articulate why Kafka was exciting, it wouldn't succeed as an open source project. The blog post explaining the "why" became the single biggest driver of adoption, outweighing all the technical work.

Tenacity beats optimism as a founder trait

Kreps doesn't consider himself an optimist. His formula: "The probability is only 20%, but we're not going to give up until we've lost all hope." Not inspiring as a slogan, but effective as a operating principle. Pain tolerance and determination compensate for lack of natural optimism.

Counter-Intuitive

Confluent bet on both software and cloud when everyone said pick one

Advisors told them to choose: enterprise software or cloud. Splitting a new market in half didn't make sense, so they pursued both despite the impossible engineering burden. They built the software product first (faster to ship), then pulled resources away to build the cloud offering, losing early customers along the way.

"We just kind of bludgeoned our way through. None of it was pretty. Some of our biggest early customers quit on us."

The CEO must lean into the things that will kill the company

The natural tendency is to avoid talking about existential threats. Kreps argues the CEO's job is the opposite: identify the things that could kill the company and address them head-on, even when nobody else wants to discuss them.

Data Points

Amazon's Kinesis was built in imitation of Kafka

The competitive threat was real and multi-pronged: Amazon built an incompatible clone (Kinesis), and if that didn't capture the market, they'd wrap open source Kafka itself. This galvanized Confluent's urgency to ship a managed service.

Future-Looking

The CEO role demands becoming the most multidisciplinary person in the room

Kreps argues that even executives brought in from other disciplines need to become deeply curious across every function. The engineering habit of continuous learning transfers well, but the breadth required goes far beyond any single discipline.

How Francois Chollet Is Building A New Path To AGI
Y Combinator
Core

Endia is rebuilding the entire ML stack, not making another coding agent

Chollet's new lab Endia is doing program synthesis research — building a new learning substrate that's fundamentally different from parametric deep learning. Instead of fitting parameters via gradient descent, they're building an alternative to deep learning itself, working at the lowest level of the ML stack.

Don't commit to foundations too early, but make sure you're compounding

It took Endia six months of trying different approaches before finding solid foundations. The key principle: you don't want to constantly try new things that don't reuse learnings. Once you find the right foundation, build a compounding stack — layer on layer, each building on the last.

ARC-AGI benchmarks target the residual gap in AI capabilities

The benchmark isn't a fixed "if you pass this, it's AGI" test. It's a moving target that identifies what current frontier models still can't do compared to humans, deliberately targeting those gaps. Each new version gets harder as AI improves.

Counter-Intuitive

ARC-3 games use no language, symbols, or cultural knowledge

The 250+ games built by a dedicated game studio strip away everything that could be memorized or pattern-matched from training data. No arrows, no green-means-go, no cultural symbols. Just core knowledge priors: basic physics, objects, agents with intentions. It's interactive IQ testing.

You can't stop AI progress, so ride the wave

Despite being one of AI's most prominent critics of hype, Chollet's practical stance is pragmatic: AI progress is here, it's accelerating, and resisting it is futile. The question is how to leverage it. This from someone building an alternative to deep learning.

Data Points

ARC-3 has 250+ original games built by a dedicated game studio

Each game takes ~10 minutes for a human to play from scratch. The private test set is deliberately much harder than the public set, and uses different game concepts to resist the targeting strategies that cracked ARC-2.

Chollet estimates AGI around 2030

Based on current trajectories, around the time ARC-6 or ARC-7 is released. This from someone who has been deeply skeptical of overpromising timelines, giving the estimate more weight.

Future-Looking

ARC-5 will test compounding curriculum learning over long timescales

Future versions will have fewer games but many more levels that compound — each level requires reusing what was learned before. ARC-4 moves to continual learning; ARC-5 extends this further. The benchmarks are evolving toward testing genuine intelligence rather than pattern matching.

From Skeptic to True Believer: How OpenClaw Changed My Life
Lenny's Podcast
Core

The biggest mistake with AI agents is expecting one agent to do everything

Claire Vo runs nine specialized OpenClaw agents across three computers. The breakthrough: don't throw any task at a single agent. Build specialized ones — Sam for sales, Howie for meeting prep, Polly for family logistics. Each agent gets a narrow domain and tuned instructions.

The highest bandwidth API is just talking to it

"Ramble Mode" — instead of thinking about APIs and integrations, just describe what you want in natural language, preferably via voice notes on your phone. Don't start with "is there a Google API for this?" Start with "here's my messy inbox problem, can you fix it?"

Agent value = making the user look good, not just completing tasks

The Howie agent sends friendly morning reminders with context for each meeting, links to LinkedIn profiles, and a hype-up message. It's not just task automation — it makes Claire look better to customers and show up better for her family. This reframes agent design from "replace labor" to "amplify the person."

Counter-Intuitive

Eight hours of painful setup and a deleted calendar made her a true believer

Claire's first OpenClaw install took 8 hours and deleted her family calendar. But between the disasters, there was enough "joy and utility" to see the potential. The willingness to push through catastrophic failure to find the signal is what separates power users from skeptics.

"It just hit me with enough joy and enough utility when it wasn't deleting my calendar that I knew something was there."

A Mac Mini running free open-source agents replaced $10K/year of human labor

Claire was paying someone 10 hours/week to do sales prospecting. The Sam agent now handles it on a Mac Mini. She iterates on Sam's behavior through natural conversation: "handle international end to end, but always bring me SF-based startups." No CRM automations or no-code tools needed.

Data Points

9 agents across 3 computers, each with a name and personality

Polly, Finn, Max, Howie, Kelly, Holly, Sam, and more. Each has a distinct role: Sam handles sales, Howie preps meetings, others manage family logistics. The naming and personality aren't gimmicks — they make the system's behavior more legible and tunable.

Future-Looking

The non-commercialized "co-experience" of open source AI agents won't last

Claire notes that OpenClaw's open-source, non-commercial nature creates a uniquely positive user experience. But she recognizes this is temporary: the co-experience "doesn't feel commercialized yet" and makes her want to go "commercialize a bunch of stuff" herself. The window of pure utility without monetization pressure is closing.

Nurturing Your Mind in Public
Dialectic
Core

Intellectual discovery is a birthright, not a credential

Celine Nguyen, a product designer by day, argues that producing your intellectual worldview isn't reserved for scholars, academics, or critics. Everyone has a right — and should feel invited — to cultivate a life of the mind. This isn't anti-expertise; it's anti-gatekeeping.

"As a human being, intellectual discovery and gratification are your birthright."

Great writers create the taste by which they are relished

Borrowing from Wordsworth: the audience for your interests may be small today, but good writing can expand the market. Just as tech startups create demand for products nobody asked for, intellectual writers can shape how people relate to ideas they didn't know they cared about.

Consumption breeds creation — the fan fiction pipeline

People who consume great work are moved to create their own. Fan fiction writers become novelists. This isn't a degradation of culture; it's the natural mechanism by which creative communities grow. The more people read, the more they want to make.

Counter-Intuitive

The case for Proust is gossip, sex, and intrigue — not literary importance

Nguyen's newsletter "No One Told Me About Proust" makes the case for 3,000 pages of modernist literature not through prestige but through what people already love: gossip, the intricacies of people's romantic and sexual lives, social dynamics. Meeting people where they are, not where literary culture says they should be.

Being impulsive is a skill for psychologically load-bearing projects

For projects you've overthought — lifelong dreams you're afraid of getting wrong — the only way to start is by seizing the moment. Planning more will make you choke. Understanding your own psychology means knowing when spontaneity is the only viable strategy.

Virgil Abloh was best understood as a philosopher, not a designer

Like Christopher Alexander, certain practitioners produce work that matters, but their thinking is the real contribution. Abloh's self-reflexive approach is applicable across disciplines far beyond fashion or design.

Future-Looking

Getting older is becoming the self you want to be

Against the cultural fetishization of youth: Nguyen looked up to people in their 30s, 40s, and 50s doing amazing work and realized they just had time. Decades of practice, not youthful brilliance, produced their best work. Aging is the process of becoming, not declining.

You don't need permission — take it

Echoing Virgil Abloh: don't wait for someone to validate your intellectual or creative work. The only permission that matters is the one you give yourself. The benefit of youth is not knowing what you don't know — but that boldness can be cultivated at any age.
